# What is Balinese culture like? A journey to the essence of the Island of the Gods


To answer this question, we traveled for a month and a half to the island of Bali in Indonesia, to get to know its traditions, its religion and its way of seeing the world.

## Introduction

I close my eyes and enjoy the warm breeze coming from the Balinese jungle; it gently brushes my face and reminds me how lucky I am. The sound of the waterfall keeps time with the birds and the insects, creating a sublime melody. We arrive at the door of our accommodation, a *guest house* where a kind local family welcomes us happily into their home.

After a few days in the center of Ubud, walking its lively, noisy streets and discovering the Ubud Palace, the Monkey Forest and the nearby temples, we began the second stage of our trip. For me, this means writing down all the stories I've been going over in my mind and keep in my heart with great affection. On the outskirts of Ubud we found the perfect place, one that combines beauty and calm with the chance to meet our digital work goals. Joy bursts in our hearts, and that's why I'm sharing here what I've learned so far about the fascinating Balinese culture.

## From the "Island of the Gods" to the "Island of a Thousand Temples"

Bali is popularly known as the **Island of the Gods** because of its constant spiritual devotion and the belief that deities and spirits live in every corner of nature. However, it's also nicknamed the **Island of a Thousand Temples**, a name that is not an exaggeration at all.

### The system of the three community temples (Kahyangan Tiga)

In Bali there is no village without sanctuaries. Beyond the small family shrines that every home keeps in its courtyard, every traditional village (*Desa Adat*) is structured in a sacred way around the concept of **Kahyangan Tiga** (the three community temples), dedicated to the Hindu trinity (*Trimurti*):

1. **Pura Puseh (the Temple of Origin):** Dedicated to the god *Brahma* (the creator). It faces the sacred mountains (*Kaja*) and honors the founding ancestors of the village.
2. **Pura Desa (the Temple of the Community):** Dedicated to the god *Vishnu* (the preserver). It stands in the center of the village and is the space where neighbors gather to settle community matters and celebrate collective rites.
3. **Pura Dalem (the Temple of Death and Reincarnation):** Dedicated to the god *Shiva* (the destroyer/transformer). It is located toward the sea or the lowest part (*Kelod*), near the cemeteries.

## Spirituality and traditions in everyday life

### Daily devotion: the meaning of the offerings

<div class="post-img-box img-pequeño img-derecha"><img src="/img/diario/archivo-pegado.webp" alt="Imagen de bitácora"><span class="img-caption">Daily Balinese offerings, Canang Sari</span></div>

The Balinese people surprised us with their charm and kindness. They have a different paradigm of life: faithful to their traditions, they perform their rituals of gratitude and connection several times a day in their family temples. These offerings, known as *Canang Sari*, are the spiritual heartbeat of the island. Each one is a small hand-woven basket made of banana or palm leaves, filled with flowers whose colors depend on the sacred direction: white to the east, red to the south, yellow to the west, and blue or green to the north.

They are always accompanied by smoking incense, which they briefly waft around with a subtle movement of the hand, a gesture meant to purify the space before raising the prayer. In addition, on certain occasions after the prayer, grains of moist rice (*Bija*) are placed on the forehead or chest, symbolizing the seed of life and divine blessing.

## Gods, demons and music in the streets

While walking, it's common to be surprised by masks and stone statues that look like guardian demons. Balinese Hinduism is unique; although it shares deities like Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva with the Hinduism of India, its syncretism is its own. Since they don't try to represent the gods in human form, they turn to the forces of nature and to mythology.

<div class="post-img-box img-completo img-centro"><img src="/img/diario/dsc_0555.webp" alt="Imagen de bitácora"><span class="img-caption">Representation of gods in animal form, Balinese Hinduism</span></div>

It's frequent to be walking down the street and come across the *Banjar*, the neighborhood's community organization. A group of young people walks by with their traditional instruments (the *Gamelan*: metallophones, gongs and drums) to offer blessings and receive small coins. Among them stands out the *Barong*, a protective figure (a long costume with a monkey or tiger face, among others) that dances to the rhythm of the percussion, operated by two people inside. Families come out to accompany them, and children clap and cheer from the sidewalks.

## The Kuningan experience: when the ancestors return to the spirit world

I was lucky to arrive in Bali close to *Kuningan*, a celebration that takes place every 210 days according to the Balinese cycle. This festival marks the end of the *Galungan* cycle, and it is said to be the moment when the ancestors return to the spirit world after visiting their families.

The word *Kuningan* comes from *kunyit* (turmeric), the star of the yellow rice that is prepared for the gods and offered with coconut on banana leaves. The ceremony symbolizes the victory of *Dharma* (good) over *Adharma* (chaos). In the days before, the neighborhood was transformed: families prepared decorations together with coconut leaves, banana leaves and flowers.

<div class="post-img-box img-mediano img-izquierda"><img src="/img/diario/whatsapp-image-2026-06-29-at-12.17.48-1-.webp" alt="Imagen de bitácora"><span class="img-caption">Ngelawang, a march through the village during Galungan and Kuningan</span></div>

On the day of the ceremony, in the morning, the women walked to the neighborhood temple carrying the *Gebogan* (giant towers of fruit and flowers) on their heads. The rest of the day went by between visits to the neighbors' temple and ceremonies at their own family temples. In the afternoon, a big march of people dressed in white, with their *sarongs* and shiny blouses, went through the streets amid greetings and conversations. They kept each other company, laughed and joked among themselves; every older woman invited the neighborhood's babies, who were watching from their parents' arms, to join in, because, as Wayan told us: "In every neighborhood we know each other, we are friends and we look after one another."

Enchanted, I walked and watched how the ceremony unfolded. Leading the march was the *Barong* (the protective figure of the village that brings blessings), who stopped every few blocks; the women made offerings to him through a ritual and he danced, giving out blessings to the sound of the *Gamelan*. Being part of something so big felt incredible, and it also made me reflect on the sense of community. In Bali, this is not an abstract concept or an idea: it's a tradition, a living practice.

Everyone takes care of their surroundings and of each other. Neighbors organize themselves with rotating roles to handle the maintenance and needs of the community, Wayan told us. Rituals, care, family and tradition are a deeply integrated part of Balinese culture. Honestly, I found it very beautiful and inspiring; seeing people in the street laughing, chatting, often working, but enjoying being together, confirmed what I felt before this trip: community saves us, generates joy and prevents that existential emptiness, that loneliness we talk about so much in the West.

## The heart of the home and the family structure

The architecture of traditional homes reflects this philosophy of harmony. A family compound is not a single closed house, but several open-air pavilions. They always have a *Sanggah*, the private family temple facing the sacred mountain. When you walk through the gate, it's common to run into the *aling-aling*, a small wall that blocks the entrance to confuse evil spirits, since they are believed to be able to move only in a straight line.

The family is the axis. Siblings are named according to their birth order:

* **Wayan:** The eldest brother or sister.
* **Kadek (or Made):** The second.
* **Komang (or Nyoman):** The third.
* **Ketut:** The fourth.

In every Balinese home we've been in, a whole family lives together: grandmothers, mothers, uncles, cousins and children. One thing is clear: family life in Bali is a cultural and symbolic axis.

Doing a bit of research, we found that the culture is marked by a strong patrilineal tradition. In the rituals we know as "marriage" (*Mepejati*), the bride formally says goodbye to her ancestors in the family temple where she grew up and is "received," or adopted, in the ancestral temple of her husband's family. From that moment on, she belongs to his lineage and takes on religious and social responsibilities in her new home and *banjar*. It's more than a change of address: it's a spiritual and social transition. However, there is an exception (a beautiful one, in my opinion): when a family has only daughters, the *Nyentana* ritual is held, in which the groom moves into the woman's home, preserving the lineage and the care of the family's elders. It's an example of how the culture adapts to take care of its own.

## Resilience behind the smile: the history of Indonesia and the Balinese spirit

Chatting with Roi, a local guide from the island of Java whom we met when we arrived, we came to understand that so much warmth is also the product of a history of resistance. He told us how Indonesia was colonized and enslaved by the Dutch for more than 300 years, a period of massive extraction of resources that allowed the Netherlands to position itself as one of the most influential economic and trading powers in Europe.

After this period came the Japanese occupation (1942-1945), which was a very harsh time, although he calls it "better" in terms of the knowledge it gave the local population. I found his view interesting, that of someone who sees the past as a scar from which something was learned. But when we looked a little deeper, we found that Bali has a chapter of its own: the Dutch did not complete their conquest of the island until 1906-1908. At the beginning of the 20th century, faced with the Dutch invasion, the Balinese people carried out the *Puputan*: seeing the Dutch ships arrive, the nobility and their subjects performed a ritual mass suicide, preferring to die rather than surrender to slavery and exploitation.

This history of resistance made the *Banjar* the heart of Bali. More than a community structure, it's an invisible network of support where rituals and care of the territory are taken on as a collective commitment.

## The open secret: community life (Banjar)

In a Western world fragmented by isolation, Bali offers an interesting reflection. Perhaps the secret of its happiness is a practice in plain sight: keeping and valuing togetherness as part of everyday life, whether through a spontaneous chat on the sidewalk, by sharing work, or in shared ritual and ceremony. Perhaps Balinese happiness is not an individual pursuit but a consequence; an invitation to remember that nobody lives in the world alone and that everything takes shape in relationship with others.

## Myths, preconceptions and environmental challenges in Bali

Before traveling, it's normal to arrive with preconceived ideas shaped by social media or commercial guidebooks. However, the real Bali is much more complex and fascinating:

### Myth 1: "Bali is just a postcard-perfect beach paradise"

The reality is that Bali is a volcanic island. Although the south (Uluwatu) stands out for its dramatic cliffs and epic surf waves, the beaches in the east and north (like Amed) have black volcanic sand and calm waters that are ideal for snorkeling. The true spiritual heart of the island is not on the beach but in the jungle, the volcanoes and the rice fields. You can still find spectacular beaches in areas like Nusa Penida or [Virgin Beach](/en/journal/what-to-do-amed-bali-real-guide-budget/).

### Myth 2: "It's a purely commercial, hyper-touristy destination"

Although areas in the south concentrate massive beaches and nightclubs, the authentic Bali hasn't disappeared: **you have to go out and look for it**. All it takes is moving 15 or 20 minutes away from the main avenues to find rural tranquility, small family homes and an intact pace of life. It also depends a lot on the areas you choose to visit: for example, the [Amed area](/en/journal/what-to-do-amed-bali-real-guide-budget/) or Seminyak is calmer than other busier tourist areas like Kuta or Canggu. You can read more about this in our article [Traveling to Bali in 2026: requirements, transport and areas to stay.](/en/journal/travel-to-bali/)

### Myth 3: "It's a perfect retreat of peace, silence and tidiness"

It's far from the Western image of calm and perfection with white outfits and impeccable streets everywhere; the concept of spirituality and culture here is genuine, everyday and unique to this territory. Bali is vibrant, noisy in its urban centers and full of social life. If you're looking for absolute silence, the best experience is to live **Nyepi** (the Balinese Day of Silence), when the whole island turns off its lights, stops traffic and even shuts down its airport for 24 hours.

### A conscious look: environmental and social challenges

Visiting the island with respect also means recognizing its current challenges. Accelerated tourism growth has brought a serious plastic waste management problem that affects its rivers and coasts (although it should be said that this is not a problem exclusive to this island). There is also a marked socioeconomic gap between luxury villas and the simple reality of rural families, along with socioeconomic problems from scarce resources, such as boys and girls selling hair clips to pay for school materials. Although nobody is obliged to save the world, there are small actions for traveling responsibly that can make a small difference, such as consuming local, cutting down on single-use plastics, and respecting the daily routine and traditions of the people who live here.

## Practical tips for a respectful trip

* **At temples:** Covering shoulders and legs with a *sarong* is mandatory to enter any sacred space or government office, for both men and women.
* **On the streets:** Offerings are everywhere. Stepping on them intentionally or carelessly is considered disrespectful. Paying attention and doing a little dance to avoid them is a necessary practice.
* **Accommodation:** Booking family-run homes or *guest houses* gives you a more authentic experience and directly helps the local economy.

## Frequently asked questions about Balinese culture (FAQ)

### What happens if I accidentally step on an offering in Bali (Canang Sari)?

Apologize respectfully with a smile (a *'Suksma'* or a kind gesture with your hand) 🙏. Don't worry too much, and try to watch where you walk, especially near the entrances of homes and shops.

### Can I take part in and take photos during religious ceremonies and marches?

Yes, in our experience they are very hospitable and open to sharing their festivities. In public processions and rituals you can even join in by wearing the right clothing (*sarong* and covered shoulders). However, access to temples is generally not allowed during celebrations.

### What religion and language are spoken in Bali?

Unlike the rest of Indonesia (predominantly Muslim), more than 86% of Bali's population practices **Balinese Hinduism**. As for languages, the official language of education and commerce is **Indonesian** (*Bahasa Indonesia*), but in everyday life and within the family everyone speaks **Balinese** (*Bahasa Bali*).
